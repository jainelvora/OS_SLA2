Theory, Mathematical Proofs & Limitations

Disk Scheduling: The Mechanical Bottleneck

In traditional magnetic hard disk drives (HDDs), mechanical head positioning is orders of magnitude slower than semiconductor RAM or CPU execution:

  • Seek Time: The physical time required for the actuator arm to accelerate, travel, and settle the read/write head over the target cylinder. (Typically 4–10 ms).
  • Rotational Latency: The time required for the target sector on the spinning magnetic platter to rotate under the head. (Typically 2–4 ms at 7200 RPM).
  • Transfer Time: The electronic data throughput once the head is correctly positioned.

Because seek time dominates total I/O service time, disk scheduling algorithms re-order pending I/O requests to minimize total head traversal, boost aggregate throughput, and guarantee equitable latency bounds.

LOOK vs C-LOOK Principles

  • LOOK (Elevator Algorithm): The head scans in the active direction serving requests until reaching the last request in that direction, then reverses direction immediately (unlike SCAN, it does not travel redundantly to cylinder 0 or disk edge).
  • C-LOOK (Circular LOOK): The head scans unidirectionally. Upon servicing the furthest request, it executes an immediate return jump to the lowest request on the opposite side without servicing requests on the rewind path. This provides uniform waiting times and eliminates starvation of outer-track requests.

Core Mathematical Formulas

  • Total Head Movement (THM):
    THM = ∑ |Tracki - Tracki-1| from Head to all N requests.
  • Average Seek Distance (ASD):
    ASD = THM / N
  • Cumulative Completion Distance: The total head movement traversed from the start when request i is successfully fulfilled.
  • Maximum Completion Distance: The completion distance of the worst-case request; serves as the primary quantitative indicator of algorithmic fairness.

Theorem: The Span of Requests Behind the Head

For any static request queue moving rightwards with initial head position h:

Let U = max(requests ≥ h), L = min(requests < h), and p = max(requests < h).

  • LOOK Traversal: Serves upper requests to U, reverses, and sweeps down to L.
    THMLOOK = (U - h) + (U - L) = 2U - h - L.
  • C-LOOK Traversal (Jump Counted): Serves upper requests to U, jumps from U to L, and sweeps upwards to p.
    THMC-LOOK = (U - h) + (U - L) + (p - L) = (2U - h - L) + (p - L).

Subtracting the two equations yields:

C-LOOK − LOOK = p − L

Where p - L is the exact span of requests situated behind the head. Consequently, on any static queue, C-LOOK can never achieve lower THM than LOOK; they tie if and only if no requests lie behind the head (p = L = 0).

Real-World Mechanical & Hardware Limitations

  • Static Queue Assumption: The classic textbook model assumes all I/O requests are known at time t=0. In real operating systems, requests arrive stochastically following Poisson or bursty distributions. Under continuous arrival, LOOK can cause starvation near platter boundaries, whereas C-LOOK maintains a consistent cycle period.
  • Non-Linear Seek Actuation: The simulation calculates seek time as directly proportional to cylinder displacement (Seek ∝ ΔTrack). In physical voice-coil actuators, head motion involves rapid acceleration, constant-velocity coasting, and delicate servo-track deceleration settling. Short seeks are dominated by settling time, while long seeks approach a square-root proportionality curve (Time ∝ √Distance).
  • Rotational Latency Neglected: Traditional algorithms ignore rotational angle. Modern enterprise drives utilize Shortest Access Time First (SATF) firmware, optimizing for combined seek distance and rotational latency.
  • Solid State Drives (SSDs & NVMe): Flash-based SSDs possess no mechanical actuator arm or rotating magnetic platter. Uniform electronic access across flash blocks renders cylinder scheduling algorithms obsolete; modern OS kernels employ multi-queue NVMe schedulers (e.g. blk-mq, Kyber, BFQ) focusing on write amplification, wear leveling, and host-controller parallelism.
